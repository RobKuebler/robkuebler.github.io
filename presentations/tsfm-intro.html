The status quo

How we forecast today

1 · Collect

history of this series

2 · Fit

ARIMA, ETS, Prophet, LightGBM with lags…

3 · Tune

features, seasonality, hyperparameters

4 · Repeat

for every new problem: stores, SKUs, sensors

It works well, but every new problem starts from zero.

And a new product with 3 weeks of history? Good luck.

What if we could skip the training entirely?

Classical forecasting means one model per series or per dataset, plus a lot of tuning, and cold start is painful. Foundation models promise something simpler: you download a model, give it your history and get a forecast, without training anything yourself.

The core idea

A TSFM is an LLM for numbers

prompt ↔ context tokens ↔ values (or patches of values) writing text ↔ forecasting

Why should this work on your sales? Trend, seasonality and noise look alike across sales, energy, traffic and sensor data, and those shapes are what the model learns.

First the LLM you know: a prompt goes in, a plausible continuation comes out. Next click: a TSFM does the same with numbers. The context is the history, and the continuation is the forecast, and most current TSFMs put an uncertainty band around it. Next click: the mapping. The model was pre-trained on huge amounts of data and you use it on data it has never seen, without training it yourself, which is what people mean by "zero-shot". Last click, for the skeptics: the model has never seen your data, but it has seen millions of series with trends, weekly and yearly cycles, and noise, and those shapes repeat across domains.

2022 → 2026

Two ways to build a TSFM

Click 1, the first school: we already have giant LLMs, so why not reuse them for numbers? Click 2: in 2024 a NeurIPS paper ripped the LLM out of three of these models (GPT4TS, Time-LLM, CALF) and got the same or better accuracy for a fraction of the compute. Click 3, the second school: an LLM-like architecture, pre-trained on time series. Most of the progress since then happened here.

LLMTime · Oct 2023

Attempt 2: make the numbers readable for GPT-3

151, 162, 175 → 151,162,175,183…

Space out the digits so the tokenizer makes one token per digit. Then sample continuations and parse them back to numbers.

The surprise

It worked: zero-shot GPT-3 and LLaMA-2 matched or beat purpose-built models on standard benchmarks.

The plot twist

GPT-4 could forecast worse than GPT-3: its number tokenizer hurts, and alignment tuning (RLHF) likely hurts calibration.

So: billions of parameters trained on the internet (Reddit included), now forecasting your electricity load. What could go wrong?

Gruver, Finzi, Qiu, Wilson. Large Language Models Are Zero-Shot Time Series Forecasters. NeurIPS 2023.

LLMTime is a clever paper and the authors were careful. GPT-3's tokenizer chops numbers in weird ways, so they put spaces between the digits, and it worked surprisingly well. Chat-tuned models did worse than their base versions, though (LLaMA-2 chat vs. LLaMA-2), and the authors suspect alignment tuning like RLHF hurt GPT-4's calibration. Side note: their "GPT-3" was text-davinci-003, which is itself instruction-tuned. And with the GPT models, every forecast costs API calls.

The input adapter maps chunks of the series into the LLM's input space, and the output adapter turns the LLM's output back into numbers. Only these two small parts are trained.

Lots of papers and GitHub stars, but only small ablations checked whether the LLM was doing anything.

Time-LLM (ICLR 2024) and GPT4TS / One Fits All (NeurIPS 2023) take a pretrained LLM (LLaMA-7B for Time-LLM, the first 6 layers of GPT-2 for GPT4TS) and keep it frozen. A small input adapter turns short chunks of the series into something the LLM can read, like word embeddings, and a small output layer turns the result back into a forecast. Only those two parts are trained. GPT4TS also fine-tunes a few tiny parts of the LLM: the layer norms and positional embeddings. GPT4TS did run a small ablation without the GPT-2 layers, on a few datasets, and the LLM version won there. It made a nice story and got a lot of attention.
Remove the LLM.
Replace it with one attention layer
or with nothing at all.

Accuracy: the same or better. Compute: up to 1000× less.

Tan, Merrill, Gupta, Althoff, Hartvigsen. Are Language Models Actually Useful for Time Series Forecasting? NeurIPS 2024 (spotlight).

Three popular LLM-based forecasters (OneFitsAll/GPT4TS, Time-LLM, CALF), ablated: remove the LLM, replace it with a single attention layer, or with one randomly initialised transformer block. Results stayed the same or improved, so the LLM was mostly an expensive passenger. Training Time-LLM took 3,003 minutes on one dataset versus about 2 for the ablation; at inference, the LLM versions were 1.2× to 28× slower. If you want a foundation model for time series, train it on time series.

Part 2

Chronos, step by step

How to turn a language model into a forecaster

Chronos · step 1 of 4

1Scale: make all series look alike

raw: passengers, kWh, €, …

divided by mean |value| of the context

Sales in millions and kWh in thousands now live on the same scale: average size 1.

Text has a fixed vocabulary, but numbers don't: 0.003 and 3 million can both show up. So the first step is mean scaling, dividing every value by the mean absolute value of the context. The shape stays the same and only the y-axis changes. It is not standardisation, though: a series hovering around zero gets blown up, and one sitting at 20 ± 2 gets squashed to about 1.

Chronos · step 2 of 4

2Quantize: numbers become tokens

Chop the value range [-15, 15] into ~4,000 bins.

Each value is replaced by its bin number. That number is the token.

Vocabulary: 4,096 tokens (bins + PAD/EOS). Context: up to 512 tokens. Sounds familiar?

The bins are centred on 0, so 0 is around token 2050 and typical scaled values sit a bit above. The loss treats neighbouring bins as unrelated; the model learns that they are close from the data.

Illustration uses 10 bins so you can see them; token ids follow the real Chronos scheme.

This is uniform binning: each scaled value lands in one bin, and the bin index becomes the token id. The bins run from -15 to 15, so a value of 0 sits in the middle, around token 2050, and scaled values near 1 land a bit above it. You lose a bit of precision, but in exchange a time series is now a sentence of token ids that any language model can read. Someone usually asks whether the model knows that bin 2101 is next to 2102. The cross-entropy loss doesn't: it treats them as unrelated classes, so the model has to learn the ordering from the data.

Chronos · step 3 of 4

3Train a plain language model

214821692194221822252218 →
T5
encoder–decoder
→ 2201?

Architecture

Off-the-shelf T5. No time-series tricks at all. Sizes from 8M to 710M parameters.

Loss

Cross-entropy on the next token, the same objective LLMs use.

Consequence

The model outputs a probability for each of the 4,096 tokens, not one number.

The architecture is plain T5; the scaling and the binning are the only parts specific to time series. Because the model is a classifier over bins, it gives you a distribution for free, and that distribution can even be multimodal.

Chronos · step 4 of 4

4Sample many futures, read off quantiles

20 sampled continuations

median + 10–90% band

Undo the binning and the scaling → a probabilistic forecast, out of the box.

This works like an LLM generating text: sample a token, append it, sample the next one. Do that 20 times and you have 20 possible futures. The median is the point forecast and the quantiles give the uncertainty band. Finally, multiply back by the scale.

Chronos · the data

Where do you get "internet-scale" time series?

Public datasets

Energy, traffic, weather, retail, finance… a lot, but far from internet-scale.

TSMixup

Mix random series from different datasets into new ones. Data augmentation.

KernelSynth

Make up series from scratch by combining Gaussian process kernels.

There's no "Common Crawl" for time series, so Chronos mixes real data with augmented and fully synthetic data. KernelSynth picks a few GP kernels (linear, periodic, smooth), combines them with + and ×, and samples from the result. A small dose of synthetic data (about 10%) clearly helped; more than that hurt. Later models lean on it much more: Chronos-2 learns its multivariate skills entirely from synthetic data.

Try it yourself

Zero-shot forecast in ~10 lines


import pandas as pd
from chronos import BaseChronosPipeline   # pip install chronos-forecasting

pipeline = BaseChronosPipeline.from_pretrained("amazon/chronos-2", device_map="cpu")

df = pd.read_csv(
    "https://autogluon.s3.us-west-2.amazonaws.com/datasets/timeseries/misc/AirPassengers.csv"
)
forecast = pipeline.predict_df(
    df,
    prediction_length=36,
    quantile_levels=[0.1, 0.5, 0.9],
    id_column="item_id",
    timestamp_column="Month",
    target="#Passengers",
)

There's no .fit() and no feature engineering, and it runs on a laptop CPU.

Also available through AutoGluon and sktime.

That's all of it. You load the pretrained model, hand it a dataframe and get quantiles back. This is Chronos-2, the current version. There's no fit call because the training already happened at Amazon.

TimesFM-3, Timestral and TiRex-2 now also do multivariate + covariates + quantiles. Toto 2.0 is multivariate.

Chronos-2 model card, huggingface.co/amazon/chronos-2

Early models were univariate only: one series in, one forecast out, and no external information. That was the main reason practitioners stuck with LightGBM. Current models take related series and covariates as context, a bit like few-shot prompting in LLMs, and they output quantiles directly, which is much faster than sampling. Learning across series is off by default; turn it on with cross_learning=True.

Chronos-2 · patches

Patches instead of single values

Like reading words instead of letters: the same history needs far fewer tokens, so Chronos-2 can look back up to 8,192 values.

Chronos turned every single value into its own token, so a long history meant a very long sequence. Chronos-2 cuts the series into short, non-overlapping chunks of consecutive values and turns each chunk into one token. Next click: the future is cut into patches too, and the model fills in all of them at once instead of sampling one value after another. Each patch also carries a time index and a mask that marks missing values. The patch length here is just for the picture.

Chronos-2 · inside

Two kinds of attention and a mask

Time attention looks along one series, patch by patch, past and future.

Group attention looks across the series at the same patch of time, so sales can see the planned price.

Unknown values become 0. A mask marks real values (1) and gaps (0).

Only the target gets a forecast: quantiles from 1% to 99% for every step, in one pass.

Ansari et al. Chronos-2: From Univariate to Universal Forecasting. arXiv:2510.15821, 2025.

Chronos-2 is an encoder in the style of T5 with 120M parameters, and its layers alternate between two kinds of attention. Time attention looks along one series. Group attention looks across all series in a group at the same patch. A group can be a set of related products, the variates of a multivariate series, or, like here, a target plus its covariates. Next click: at a future step, the sales row can look at the price row, and the planned price drop is right there. Next: everything that isn't known in the future window is set to zero and switched off in the mask; the mask also marks missing values in the history. Last click: only the target gets a forecast, as 21 quantiles from 1% to 99%, and the loss is only computed on the target, so the model never has to forecast the covariates. Real multivariate data is scarce, so the multivariate training data is entirely synthetic. The numbers on the slide are made up for the picture.

GIFT-Eval is Salesforce's public benchmark and leaderboard for forecasting models, and the one most of these releases point to. The top spot changes every few months. Timestral looks like MIT, but companies with more than $20M monthly revenue need a commercial license. The GIFT-Eval leaderboard now also lists "agentic" systems that ensemble several foundation models.

Before you use one

A few caveats

Benchmarks ≠ your data

Leaderboard rank may not transfer to your data. Evaluate on your series with a proper backtest.

Keep the baselines

Always compare against seasonal naive, ETS and your current model. Sometimes they win.

Leakage

Public benchmarks may overlap with pre-training data. Newer benchmarks like TIME use freshly collected data to avoid that overlap.

Check the quantiles

A band is only useful if it's calibrated. Measure coverage, not just MAPE.

TSFMs make a great default and a strong baseline. A well-tuned LightGBM with rich features may still win, but a TSFM gives you a very good forecast in five minutes, and any new model now has to beat that.
